# 36. Why These Laws and Not Others?

"Why does the electron weigh what it weighs?"

"Because that is what it weighs. Somebody measured it."

"Why that number and not another one?"

"Because there are a great many universes with other numbers in them, most of them empty, and this is one of the few with anybody in it to ask."

Those are the two answers usually on offer, for the electron and for every other number chapter one counted, the ones physics takes from laboratories and enters by hand, nineteen of them or twenty-six depending on how neutrinos are handled. The numbers are brute facts and there is nothing to say about them, or they were drawn from an enormous collection of alternatives and we see this set because the others have nobody in them to look. Both answers end the conversation somewhere the reader cannot go. The first says there is nothing behind the numbers and does not say how it knows. The second needs a shelf with the other worlds standing on it and does not say where the shelf is.

A third kind of answer was read aloud in London in the summer of 1858.

On 18 June 1858 a package reached Down House in Kent, and Charles Darwin opened it and found twenty pages of his own theory written by somebody he had never met. Alfred Russel Wallace had drafted the essay on Ternate, in the Moluccas, between bouts of fever, called it "On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely from the Original Type", and asked that it be passed to Charles Lyell if it seemed worth anything. Darwin had been assembling the same argument for twenty years, through barnacles and pigeon fanciers and a filing system of small stubborn facts, and he wrote to Lyell that day. Your words have come true with a vengeance. If Wallace had my manuscript sketch written out in 1842 he could not have made a better short abstract. All my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed.

Ten days later his son Charles Waring Darwin, eighteen months old, died of scarlet fever. On 1 July, Charles Lyell and Joseph Hooker took both accounts to the Linnean Society of London and put them before the meeting, where the society's secretary, John Joseph Bennett, read them aloud. Darwin was at Down with his family, burying the child. Wallace was in the Malay Archipelago and learned that the meeting had happened months afterward, in a letter. The reading drew no discussion.

Thomas Bell, the society's president, reviewed the year in his address the following May. The year which has passed, he said, has not indeed been marked by any of those striking discoveries which at once revolutionize the department of science on which they bear. Bell had been in the chair on the evening of 1 July.

What Bennett read aloud was a procedure for producing the appearance of design with nothing in it that chooses: variation happens, some variants leave more descendants, and the arithmetic does the rest. It has an application in physics that has nothing to do with finches, and the question it applies to is the one underneath chapter one's list: why the world runs on these rules with these numbers instead of some others.

## What a description is for

In 1950 Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau wrote down an account of superconductivity that mentions no electrons at all. They introduced one quantity standing for how much of the metal has gone superconducting at each place, expanded the energy in powers of it, and read off the two lengths a superconductor has: how far the superconducting state can vary over, and how deep a magnetic field soaks in before the metal expels it. The account worked near the temperature where superconductivity sets in and nowhere else. The constants in it were fixed by measurement, because nothing in the argument could produce them.

In 1957 John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and Robert Schrieffer explained superconductivity from underneath: electrons pair up through their coupling to the vibrations of the lattice, and the pairs move as single objects. Two years later Lev Gor'kov showed that the 1950 account is what the 1957 theory becomes near that temperature. The constants nobody had been able to calculate came out in terms of the pairing. The 1950 account did not become wrong when its reasons arrived. It stopped being the bottom and stayed in the textbooks, because near the transition it is the easier of the two to compute with and gives the same answers.

That is an **effective description**: correct inside a range, carrying constants it does not generate, and usually able to say where it will fail. Ginzburg and Landau's account announces its own ceiling, since the expansion it rests on is only good while the superconducting quantity is small, which is to say near the transition and not far below it. Physics is made of descriptions like that and there is no defeat in having one. The Standard Model is an effective description of the same kind. The complaint chapter one lodged against it was about direction rather than count. Numbers go in and predictions come out. No arrangement of it sends a number the other way.

## The planet that was easier to move

Chapter thirty-four left one leftover in the solar system: 43 seconds of arc per century in Mercury's orbit that no amount of Newtonian bookkeeping supplied, about a hundredth of a degree in a human lifetime. The nineteenth century tried to account for it with an extra planet. The man who proposed the planet had done it once before.

Urbain Le Verrier had found Neptune on paper in 1846, by taking the anomalies in the orbit of Uranus and computing where an unseen body would have to sit, and the telescope at Berlin found it that night within a degree of the position he gave. So he proposed another one, inside Mercury's orbit, hot and small and hard to see against the sun. A country doctor at Orgères-en-Beauce named Edmond Lescarbault wrote to say he had watched a dark spot cross the face of the sun in March of that year. Le Verrier travelled out to interview him, came back convinced, and the planet was named Vulcan.

Vulcan was never there. Each failed search moved it rather than killing it: smaller, closer in, differently tilted, and in one version broken into a belt of bodies each too small to be seen, which no observation could exclude. Nothing in the proposal forbade those moves, because a planet comes with a mass and an orbit and both are dials. During the total eclipse of 29 July 1878 two experienced American observers, James Craig Watson at Separation in Wyoming and Lewis Swift near Denver, both reported a reddish object near the sun. The two positions did not agree with each other. Nobody else at that eclipse saw anything. Eclipse expeditions went on looking through 1908.

In November 1915 Einstein computed the same 43 seconds of arc from field equations with nothing adjustable anywhere in them.

Vulcan accommodated everything and predicted nothing, and closing the search took fifty-six years. Flexibility is what lets a description survive contact with data. Every bit of it is subtracted from what the description tells you. Chapter one counted the inputs. Each measured number a description absorbs is a fact about the world that description will never predict. Le Verrier's planet had two knobs, a mass and an orbital radius, and between them they could reproduce any perihelion shift anybody came back with, which is why no measurement of the perihelion shift could ever have got rid of it. The trade stops paying at the point where nothing anybody could see would tell against the description, and Vulcan reached it when the belt version arrived. From then on the only property the planet had left was that it could be moved. Einstein's equations had none of that, which is why 43 seconds of arc could have killed them.

## What an assumption costs

In 1978 Jorma Rissanen published a paper called "Modeling by shortest data description". Its rule for choosing between accounts of the same data is to add two lengths: what it takes to write the account down, and what it takes to write down the data once you have the account. Take whichever account makes the two together smaller. Rissanen's name for that sum is the description length. An explanation is a compression. A rule that costs more to state than it saves has explained nothing.

The inverse electromagnetic coupling read at rest is 137.035999177. The shared-width trial map in chapter twenty-nine returns 137.035660, about two and a half parts per million away. Its certified error bounds exclude numerical rounding as the explanation, while the missing physical matching has not been shown to remove the mismatch.

The package from Ternate is the same accounting seen from the other end. What Darwin had been accumulating for twenty years was data: island species, barnacle anatomy, fossil beds, the breeding records of pigeon fanciers who had made fantails and pouters and tumblers out of the rock dove, geographical ranges that made no sense on any other reading. Stored as itself, that is a library. The sentence that variants which leave more descendants become more common adds no fact to the library. It shortens it, by removing the need to hold each adaptation as a separate item. That shortening is why two men at opposite ends of the world wrote the same sentence out of two entirely different collections of animals.

## Reading the collection as a sieve

String theory gave up point particles. Its known difficulty is the count. Curling the extra dimensions is a choice, threading fluxes through them is another, and choosing bundles and branes and hidden sectors are more. The product of all those choices is an enormous collection of complete specifications for a low-energy world, each with its own particles and its own constants. Somewhere in that collection, the hope goes, is one that looks like ours. It also holds worlds with four families of matter and worlds in which the proton comes apart in a second.

A target turns the collection into a stock list. The overlap constructions of the earlier chapters supply one: the global gauge group, three colors, three families, the hypercharges, one Higgs doublet, no unwanted light matter, and no grand-unified gauge boson to take a proton apart. The exact group is the item that does most of the rejecting, because two constructions can match each other on every local property and disagree about which charges are allowed to exist at all, and a charge is the quantity an observer's records are keeping count of. Take that target to the collection and reject everything that misses it.

In 2006 [Vincent Bouchard and Ron Donagi](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0512149) published a construction that gets a long way down the list. Curl the extra dimensions on a particular six-dimensional shape, one of the kind called a Calabi-Yau and one with a loop in it that cannot be shrunk to a point, and hang on it a structure whose symmetry is the smallest grand-unified group there is. Then carry that symmetry around the unshrinkable loop and compare it with what you set out with, which is chapter twelve's round trip performed on a larger object, and the mismatch breaks the big group down to the visible one. Out of it comes the exact group, three generations, no extra light matter the experiments would have caught, and a region of the settings with exactly one Higgs pair in it. The group, the three generations, the hypercharges, the Higgs count and the absence of exotic matter all check out. Then there are the dials.

Count them. Three kinds come with the construction, one kind setting the sizes of the curled directions, one the shape they are curled into, and one the structure hung on top, and the published data for that one-Higgs region record eleven of the first, eleven of the second and forty-nine of the third. Seventy-one complex directions, a hundred and forty-two real ones, counted before anything has been done to hold the vacuum in place. The comparison against measurement uses five real numbers: two coupling strengths read at the Z boson's mass, the vacuum value of the Higgs field, the Higgs mass and the top quark's mass. The comparison consumes none of the construction's own values, so the table of how that comparison responds to the hundred and forty-two dials is a hundred and forty-two columns of zeros. The map has rank zero. Any smooth comparison that returns five numbers leaves at least a hundred and thirty-seven directions unpinned, which is a hundred and forty-two minus five and is unrelated to the hundred and thirty-seven of chapter twenty-nine.

So the construction matches the target on structure, and the published data supply no formula linking its seventy-one dials to the five numbers. The only comparison anyone can run ignores the dials, and a comparison that ignores the dials pins none of them, which is what rank zero says.

## Testing a specified world

A specified vacuum that disagrees with the data is ruled out, and the collection has an escape ready. The move available is to go and stand at another vacuum, and with a collection that size on the shelf there is always another one to stand at. Vulcan was moved every time a search came back empty. A measurement that retires one vacuum leaves the collection as full as it was.

The move is available because there is an outside: a space of alternatives sitting outside any particular world, which a description can be relocated into whenever the world it was sitting in fails to match the data.

There is no such space here. The reason is arithmetic. Chapter twenty-nine read one cell of an observer's screen twice, from the outside as an area and from the inside as the smallest electromagnetic act available, and the two readings are readings of one quantity, so setting them equal gives an equation whose unknown appears on both sides. One number satisfies it. Change that number and the two readings disagree. There is no third place to put the difference and no neighboring world to carry it off.

Exactly one comes from the property chapter twenty-nine proved about the equation. Feed it two trial grains some distance apart and it hands back two grains at most 0.0724 of that distance apart, so the passes squeeze rather than spread, and every starting guess in the domain walks to the same destination. A second destination would be a second world with a different fine-structure constant in it. The squeezing leaves nowhere for one to sit. The loop comes in the two versions chapter twenty-nine laid side by side, the bare one and the one that recomputes the weak and strong forces' shared width inside it, and each has its own single fixed point. The cell holds all three forces, so the physical loop is the second, and it lands two and a half parts per million short of the laboratory's number; the last digits are work in progress. What comes out of the equation is a reading of the architecture in the same way that the diagonal of a square is a reading of the square. Nobody chose the fine-structure constant any more than anybody chose the square root of two. The panel of dials that was supposed to have been set at the beginning is missing along with the beginning.

## Scoring a rule

Chapter nine derived the repair move from three requirements. Every move that fails one of them is a rule somebody could have written down, and rules that get written down compete.

Three quantities, each of them a count of something. The first is schedule stability. Take a starting arrangement, run a rule until it stops under one order of repairs, run it again under a different order, and ask whether the two settled readings agree; do that across many starting arrangements and many orders, and the fraction that agree is a number between zero and one. Chapter six's twelve observers ran from every one of two thousand and forty-eight states under sixteen schedules and landed every time on the same arrangement, which is that number coming out at 1.

The second is observer yield: how many observers a rule produces. A region counts as one when its record reads the same at the end of a stretch of time as it did at the beginning, and when what it holds carries information about what its boundary does next. Both halves are measurable, the first as how far the reading has drifted and the second as how much the record tells you about the neighbors' future. Count the regions that qualify and take the average over the running of the world.

The third is the cost of writing the rule down, in Rissanen's sense, and enters with a minus sign. A rule's score is the first two with positive weights, minus the third.

The three pull against each other. A rule that repairs nothing scores a perfect 1 on stability, because every order of doing nothing agrees with every other order of doing nothing. It is about as cheap to write down as a rule can be, and yields not one region holding a record about anything, so the second term deletes it. A rule that buys the second term by copying down everything it ever sees pays for the copies in the third. The rule chapter nine derived, which moves two disagreeing readings to their midpoint and touches nothing else, is where the three pulls balance.

The rules are competing for one finite thing, which is the capacity of the structure to repair itself. A rule's share is the fraction of that capacity being spent its way. A rule scoring above the average grows its share at a rate proportional to how far above the average it sits, and one below shrinks the same way. That is **replicator dynamics**, arithmetic for the sentence that fitter types spread. Nothing in it requires universes to reproduce.

Write f for a rule's score, put a bar over it for the average of the scores across the rules holding capacity, weighted by their shares, and let t measure how far the reallocation has run. Ask how that average moves as the shares move, and the answer is one line.

$$\frac{d\bar{f}}{dt} = \operatorname{Var}(f)$$

The left side is the rate at which the average score changes. The right side is the variance of the scores: the average of the squared distances of the individual scores from the average score. So the average climbs at exactly the rate at which the survivors disagree with each other, and since a squared distance is never negative, the average never falls.

Two rules make it concrete. Scores of 3 and 1, half the capacity each, and an average of 2. The first gains half a share per unit of t and the second loses the same. The variance is 1, and the average is climbing at 1. As the first takes over, the spread closes and the climb stops, because the mean rises only on disagreement and by then there is none.

What survives that is a rule whose answers do not depend on the order anybody works in, which is thick with regions that hold stable predictive records, and which is cheap to state. Chapter six measured the first of those on twelve observers, and chapter nine derived the move that supplies it.

## The rule that stays put

The competition ends with one rule holding the whole share, and inside the history that rule does not change. Chapter nineteen built a small version of it, a rule for moving units, a rule for what each move writes and a rule for what is kept and what is filed, and appendix D runs the full one, the OPH law, on a laptop. It is static. Nothing in the world revises it, because everything in the world is something it is doing.

What changes is everything the rule acts on, and the arithmetic of the last section runs again one level down, with patterns in the place of rules. A pattern's share is the fraction of the history it occupies. Whatever the rule erases loses its share, and whatever survives every repair keeps it. At the lowest layer there is no reproduction and no offspring. Selection there is persistence under a rule that never changes, and the survivors are what physics calls its laws.

Go through them. A disturbance spread across the eleven directions of a carrier is erased in the fast and middle bands within a few passes, and the slow band outlives it, so space has three directions. A total that no repair can alter survives every repair, and is a conserved charge. A twist in a loop survives every repair that does not cut the loop, and is a particle. A write that survives the next read is a record, and a record that survives every comparison is a fact. The settled background every knot is maintained against survives because it is what repair returns to, and it is the vacuum with its Higgs field pointing. None of these was designed. Each is what was left when the rule had finished erasing everything that could be erased.

Then a second round runs on the survivors. Some patterns persist by more than surviving. They repair themselves, drawing on the stable layer: knots bound into atoms that hold their shells, atoms into molecules that copy themselves, molecules into cells that keep an inside different from their outside. Those patterns have offspring, and the replicator equation applies to them with offspring in place of shares, which is Darwin's argument. Chapter thirty-eight asks why anything bothers.

The two rounds are one equation run on two kinds of pattern. The first round selected the laws of physics from everything one static rule could do. The second selects observers from everything those laws allow, and some of the observers it selects end up reading the rule back out of their own records. The laws do not evolve under the observers in turn, because they are the survivors of a rule that holds the whole share, and they are as fixed as the rule is.

## An ensemble of one

The anthropic argument runs as follows: there is a large collection of worlds with different settings, the great majority are barren, ours is one of the few in which anybody could be around to take a reading, and no further explanation of the settings is needed. As selection reasoning it is sound. It requires the collection.

Take the phrase "the largest prime number". It has the grammar of a name and there is nothing at the end of it, and Euclid's argument shows why: take any finite list of primes, multiply them together, add one, and the result is divisible by none of them, so no finite list is all of them. The phrase does not pick out a number that turned out to have some other property. It picks out nothing.

The phrase "a universe with a different fine-structure constant" is of that kind. The two readings of a cell fix that number by requiring agreement with each other. A structure whose readings disagree does not settle into a world that observers then fail to appear in. It does not settle at all, and the phrase, like the largest prime, picks out nothing.

So the ensemble was never larger than one. An anthropic filter has nothing to filter. The selection that did happen ran over repair rules rather than over worlds, and from inside.

Running from inside has a price. The capacity being competed for is the structure's own, the observers being counted include whoever is doing the counting, and the rule that takes the whole share is the rule the judges are running on. Darwin's argument reached Down House in an envelope from Ternate, carried halfway around the world by a post office, so there was a morning before it arrived and a morning after. There is no morning before this one. Nothing was posted and nothing was opened, and whatever holds the arrangement up is somewhere inside it.
