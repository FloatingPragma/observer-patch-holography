# Which particle can two observers identify?

Two preparations belong to the same observable class for a specified set of experiments if every calibrated sequence of measurements gives the same joint outcome probabilities, including refusals. Finite count banks test that equivalence to their stated resolution. An additional detector can split a class whose earlier records agreed.

A particle assignment starts with the source setting, the receiver's clicks, the outcomes when the source is off, and the intervening material and clock. A second observer calibrates their own instruments, then compares outcome distributions for the same physical preparations. The catalogue below gives the measurements available in the charged and neutral lepton search. The [full particle inventory](../../model/evaluation/SCORECARD.md#standard-model-particle-identifications) lists the remaining families and the distinct observations each requires.

## Comparing two descriptions of one experiment

If Alice describes the source by ρ, the route by U and the receiver by D, Bob's probability is tr(D UρU†). If the two endpoints independently change coordinates by A and B, these three objects become AρA†, BUA† and BDB†. The trace is unchanged. This requires matching the physical detector axes. Keeping an old component number while rotating the instrument changes the question it asks. A change of family coordinates likewise transports the family energy projectors. A gauge transformation alone cannot turn one rest energy into a different one. A refused source, an empty detector and a calibrated zero charge are different records. A source or probe absent from an experiment gives an unmeasured observable. This distinction matters for the flavour labels. A neutrino flavour refers to the charged lepton identified at its production or detection vertex. Assigning the three windings the names electron, muon and tau would supply that association as an input.
